Here's the pattern almost every community programme follows. Session one is exciting. New people, warm introductions, a sense that something is starting. Attendance is high because everyone showed up to see what this is. Session two is smaller — natural drop-off from the curious-but-not-committed. Session three is smaller again, but the ones who stay are engaged and the room has warmth.

Then something happens at session four or five. Attendance stalls. The same people say the same things. The founder starts feeling like they're carrying the room. New people who show up don't quite fit the vibe the early cohort has settled into. The programme begins to feel like maintenance instead of momentum.

By session eight the founder is quietly deciding whether to keep it running.

This is not a failure of programming. It's a failure of design. Community programmes that stall have the same shape as community programmes that compound — for the first three sessions. What separates them is what was designed *before* session one that only starts paying off at session eight.

Here's what those design choices look like.

## They design the <span class="tint-sage">arc</span>, not the event

Most community programmes are designed one session at a time. Each session has a topic, a format, a host, a run-of-show. The programme is a series of good gatherings.

Community programmes that compound are designed as an **arc** — a six-month, twelve-month, or twenty-four-month trajectory that individual sessions serve. The arc has a shape: *strangers becoming members becoming participants becoming hosts.* Each phase of the arc has its own design requirements, and the sessions inside each phase share a rhythm.

Without an arc, session eight is just another good gathering. With an arc, session eight is a specific inflection point in a designed trajectory — the moment when the earliest members start bringing the next cohort into the room, or when the format shifts from facilitator-led to member-led, or when the first real friendships form beyond the sessions themselves.

If your community programme is scheduled but not arced, session eight will feel the same as session three. If it's arced, session eight will feel like the first version of the programme starting to work the way it was designed to work.

## They design the <span class="tint-sage">membership signal</span> before the first session

Every community programme has a moment when someone shifts from *attending* to *belonging.* In programmes that stall, this moment is accidental. In programmes that compound, it's engineered.

The mechanism varies by programme. Sometimes it's a ritual — the first member to speak in a new session is always the one who invited someone new since the last one. Sometimes it's an artefact — a physical object handed to each member at their third session. Sometimes it's a name — the group has internal language ("first-timers", "regulars", "core") that only insiders know. Sometimes it's a role — after four sessions, the member co-hosts one segment of the next.

The specifics matter less than the principle: **belonging is a designed signal, not an emergent feeling.** If you want your community to compound, the moment someone becomes a member has to be something a member can feel happening to them — not something they retrospectively realise after they've been around a while.

## They design the <span class="tint-sage">ritual</span> only insiders know

Every community that lasts has at least one ritual that outsiders wouldn't recognise as ritual. A specific opening question repeated every session. A moment of silence at a specific point. A cadence of introductions in a specific order. A recurring joke that means something. A song, a food, a physical arrangement of chairs.

The purpose of the ritual is dual: it gives members something to notice about their own membership (*"we do it this way"*), and it gives them something to bring outsiders into (*"you have to be there when we do X"*).

Programmes without ritual feel like a series of one-off events. Programmes with ritual feel like a place with its own culture — and culture is what makes members bring in the next generation without being asked to. It's the same reason [moments people can retell](/thoughts/events-craft/anatomy-of-a-memorable-moment) travel further than moments they merely enjoyed.

Ritual is also what makes a community programme survive the founder stepping out of the room. Which brings us to the hardest test.

## They pass the <span class="tint-sage">founder-independence</span> test

The single hardest question a community programme has to answer is: *what happens when the founder stops being in the room?*

Most programmes fail this test. The programme was built around the founder's convening energy, their point of view, their relationships, their willingness to keep showing up. When the founder gets busy, gets sick, or wants to build something else, the programme evaporates in three sessions.

Programmes that compound have a specific test built into the design: at some pre-decided session — often session six or twelve — the founder deliberately steps back and lets members run the format. Not as a stunt. As a design commitment. The programme has been engineered so that by that session, enough members have internalised the ritual, the arc, and the belonging signal to keep it running without the founder in front of the room.

If the programme can't survive session six without the founder, the founder built a session series, not a community. Both are legitimate — but they're different products with different economics. A session series stops when the founder does. A community compounds because members carry the design forward.

## What this means for <span class="tint-sage">the brief</span>

If you're building a community programme — or you have one that's plateaued — the brief-stage questions worth asking are:

1. **What is the twelve-month arc?** Where should a member be at month one vs. month six vs. month twelve? If the answer is *"more of the same, hopefully"*, there's no arc.
2. **What's the belonging signal?** How does a member know they've moved from attendee to insider? If it's *"they'll just feel it"*, it's not designed.
3. **What ritual only insiders know?** If you can name three, you're compounding. If you can name none, you're a session series.
4. **What happens when you step back?** If the answer is *"it stops"*, the programme is fragile. If the answer is *"the design carries it"*, you've built something that survives you.

Answering those four honestly will tell you whether you're building a compounding community or a nicely-attended session series. Both are fine — but the first is a much better use of the time.
