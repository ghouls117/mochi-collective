Every event ends with the same question, asked with slightly different phrasing depending on who's paying.

"Was it worth it?"

The answer, most of the time, is a guess dressed up in numbers. Attendance figures. NPS scores from the exit survey. A slide with the words *"successful"* and *"exceeded expectations."* None of these are answers. They're placeholders.

The reason isn't that the events industry is bad at measurement. It's that most events measure the wrong things at the wrong times, using instruments designed for something else entirely. Then the report gets written, filed, and quietly forgotten — until the next event, when the same question gets asked again.

Here's the framework we use instead. It's what our [Impact Measurement practice](/impact-measurement) runs against every experience we design.

## The problem with the current measurement stack

Three habits break most event measurement before it starts.

<strong class="tint-pink">Habit 1: Measuring the wrong thing.</strong> Attendance is a logistics number, not an impact number. NPS taken thirty seconds after the closing gets you a mood reading, not a memory. Feedback forms measure what people are willing to type, not what they actually think. None of these tell you whether the experience did the job it was booked to do.

<strong class="tint-blue">Habit 2: Measuring at the wrong time.</strong> Most measurement happens within 24 hours of the event ending — the exact window where the memory is at its most inflated. Ask someone what they thought of the retreat the morning after and you'll get a warm answer. Ask them three weeks later and you'll get the truth. Different timing surfaces different signals.

<strong class="tint-sage">Habit 3: Measuring what's easy instead of what's real.</strong> Impressions, hashtag counts, and photo uploads are all measurable. They're also all proxies for a proxy of what actually matters. If you can measure it in real time, chances are you're measuring the wrong thing.

The framework that follows fixes the three habits.

## Three categories of signal — pick from all three

Every experience produces three kinds of signal after the fact. Good measurement pulls from all three, not just one.

### <span class="tint-pink">Behavioural signals</span> — what people *did* differently

This is the strongest category, and the hardest to instrument. It's not what attendees say they'll do. It's what they actually did in the days and weeks that followed.

Did the team have the difficult conversation the retreat was designed to unlock? Did the account decide to renew, or introduce us to procurement? Did the community members show up to the next unpaid gathering three months later? Did the brand activation attendees ever mention the brand again in a context that wasn't prompted?

Behavioural signals require tracking someone across time. They cost something to collect — usually a light-touch follow-up thirty days and ninety days after. But they're the only signals that answer the actual question.

### <span class="tint-blue">Commercial signals</span> — what decision moved

Every experience ladders to a downstream decision. A sales offsite ladders to a deal that closes. A wellness retreat ladders to a retention decision. A community programme ladders to a returning member. A brand activation ladders to a media buy that gets renewed.

If the experience was designed against a specific downstream decision (and it should have been — this is one of our six brief questions), then you can measure whether that decision moved. Not just whether it closed favourably — whether the experience made the decision *easier to make*. Did the pipeline shorten? Did the negotiation get less abrasive? Did the renewal come in without pushback?

Commercial signals require the decision to be named in advance. If it wasn't, this category doesn't work retroactively. Which is exactly why we push clients to name the decision at the brief stage, before we design.

### <span class="tint-sage">Cultural signals</span> — what got said unprompted

This is the softest category and often the most predictive. It's the language attendees use about the experience without being asked. In Slack messages. In LinkedIn posts they wrote themselves. In corridor conversations that get relayed back to you three weeks later. In the sentence someone says over dinner in a completely different room.

Cultural signals are hardest to instrument, but you can approximate them. Set up alerts for mentions. Ask three attendees for the sentence they've heard other attendees say. Watch which specific moments get referenced in follow-up emails. If the same phrase from the closing keeps surfacing in unrelated meetings six weeks later, the experience is alive.

## The timing map — three measurement windows

Different signals surface in different windows. Measure them all at 24 hours and you'll get noise. Measure them at the right window and you'll get a signal you can act on.

<strong class="tint-pink">7 days out.</strong> Sensory memory has settled. Fatigue has cleared. This is the window where the honest emotional read is available. Not "how would you rate the event" — but "when you think back to the day, what specific moment comes up first?" One question. Free text. Twenty responses tell you more than a hundred NPS scores.

<strong class="tint-blue">30 days out.</strong> Behavioural signals are starting to show. The follow-up meeting either happened or it didn't. The renewal conversation either got easier or it didn't. This is the window for the commercial signal check.

<strong class="tint-sage">90 days out.</strong> The peak-end memory has consolidated. What remains at 90 days is what people will still remember at 12 months. This is the window for the cultural signal — has anyone quoted the experience unprompted? Are people describing it accurately, or has the memory drifted?

Measure at these three windows, not all at once, and the picture that emerges is honest.

## What to stop measuring

Some data is worth collecting only because it's cheap — not because it's useful. In our practice, we've stopped measuring:

- **NPS taken same-day.** Mood, not memory. The score is inflated and non-actionable.
- **Photo counts and hashtag mentions.** Correlates with logistics, not impact. Popular events get photographed. So do bad ones.
- **Feedback form averages.** The 3.9-out-of-5 tells you nothing. The one comment about the specific moment that landed tells you everything.
- **Attendance numbers past the top-line.** Whether 950 or 1,050 people showed up doesn't change the answer to whether the experience did its job. It only changes the catering invoice.

Stopping measurement is a discipline. It frees up the effort to measure the three things that actually matter.

## The one metric that beats the others

If we could keep only one measurement instrument, we'd keep the Monday sentence.

At the brief stage, we work with clients to write, in advance, the sentence they hope attendees will say to each other on the Monday after the experience. Not a marketing tagline. Not a soundbite. The actual sentence — informal, unprompted, said in a corridor or over Slack.

Then, seven to fourteen days after the experience, we go looking for it. Not the exact phrase. The *approximate* sentence. Did anyone say something close to what we predicted? Was the emotional shape of the sentence right?

If yes — the experience worked. If no — the experience missed, and we can usually tell you where.

It's a single question. It costs almost nothing to check. And it correlates more strongly with whether the experience produced downstream commercial signals than any other metric we've tried. Vanity metrics collapse. The Monday sentence holds.

## The uncomfortable truth about measurement

Good measurement often reveals that an event you were proud of didn't do the job. And that an event you weren't sure about did.

This is the reason most measurement stays vanity-oriented — real measurement can embarrass the organiser. But without it, you'll keep making the same design mistakes at every event, retreat, or activation you commission. And you won't know why the ones that worked, worked.

The only way out is to instrument the experience against real signals, at real windows, and to look honestly at what comes back.
